## What military ranges add to the evidence

Military training areas are not ordinary pieces of sky. They host fast jets, [drones]({{ 'drones/' | relative_url }}), electronic-warfare exercises, missile testing, airborne sensors, target systems, and classified activities. Pilots operating there are deliberately exposed to unusual situations that civilian aviators rarely encounter.

As a result, observers at training ranges are more likely to see something they cannot immediately identify. A distant drone, a test article, a balloon, a sensor reflection, or an object operating outside expected parameters may all generate a report. The key point is that the environment itself contains more unusual stimuli than normal commercial airspace. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceThe director of national intelligence (DNI) is a cabinet-level United States government intelligence...</span></span></span>

Military personnel also tend to be trained observers. Fighter pilots, radar operators, intelligence specialists and sensor technicians are accustomed to noticing anomalies because their jobs require them to recognise potential hazards. That does not make them infallible, but it increases the likelihood that unusual observations will be documented rather than ignored. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aiaa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/AIAA-UAPIOC-Opinion-Paper-UAP-Occupational-Safety-Reporting_ForPublication_kb.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aiaa.org">[AIAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aiaa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Shaping the future of aerospace ADDRESSING THE UNKNOWN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shaping the future of aerospaceADDRESSING THE UNKNOWN:August 8, 2025 — Put simply, our goal is to describe the processes by which...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 8, 2025</span></span></span> - Shaping the future of aerospace

## Collection bias and sensor density

The most important official explanation for range clustering is collection bias. The 2021 U.S. intelligence assessment explicitly noted that UAP sightings tended to cluster around training and testing grounds but assessed that this could result from collection bias rather than from the locations themselves attracting anomalous objects. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceThe director of national intelligence (DNI) is a cabinet-level United States government intelligence...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, military ranges contain far more detection equipment than ordinary airspace. Aircraft carry advanced radar systems, infrared sensors, targeting pods and electronic-surveillance equipment. Ground stations and command centres may simultaneously monitor the same area. This creates a familiar problem in many fields of investigation: the more intensely an area is observed, the more anomalies appear to exist there. A city with hundreds of weather stations will record more unusual atmospheric events than a region with only one sensor, even if the underlying phenomenon is equally common.

Following increased official attention, the military introduced clearer reporting pathways and encouraged personnel to submit observations that might previously have gone unreported. ### Better sensors can create harder cases

An important paradox is that more sophisticated sensors do not always produce easier identifications.

Modern military systems often detect objects outside normal visual perception. Infrared systems, radar returns, electronic signatures and multi-sensor tracks can reveal targets that are invisible to the naked eye. Yet interpreting those signals may require specialised expertise, and sensor artefacts can sometimes resemble genuine objects. Official UAP investigations repeatedly caution that sensor error, misinterpretation and data limitations remain possible [explanations]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) in some cases. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Director_of_National_Intelligence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceThe director of national intelligence (DNI) is a cabinet-level United States government intelligence...</span></span></span>

This means that sensor-rich environments generate both more evidence and more technical ambiguity. The same conditions that make detection possible can also complicate interpretation.
